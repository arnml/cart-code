\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is commonly implemented as a fixed top-$k$ pipeline, retrieving the highest-ranked passages and passing them to the generator~\cite{klesel2025rag,zhao2026aigcsurvey,yu2024rankrag,lee2025setr,verma2026reflectiverag}. Although effective, fixed-$k$ retrieval has two important drawbacks: larger $k$ increases token-processing and generation costs, and it can expose the model to noisy or weakly relevant context~\cite{wang2024bestpractices,li2025bestpractices}. These costs are especially important in retrieval-intensive workloads, where small per-query overheads accumulate into substantial system-level expense. In the HotpotQA distractor setting, however, larger fixed retrieval budgets remain strong quality baselines: across all tested models, F1 improves monotonically from no retrieval to $k=10$. For GPT-5.4-mini, mean total tokens increase from 103.5 to 1452.5 ($14.0\times$) while F1 rises from 0.387 to 0.792; for Claude Haiku 4.5, tokens increase from 132.7 to 1639.8 ($12.4\times$) while F1 rises from 0.367 to 0.779. Thus, in this benchmark, the systems problem is not that larger $k$ is harmful on average, but that strong fixed-$k$ policies are expensive.

A natural alternative is Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, which aims to reduce cost by selecting only the most relevant passages. However, in our evaluation on GPT-5.4-mini, Adaptive-$k$ selects a compact mean $k^*=3.02$ yet achieves only 0.627 F1, underperforming fixed $k=3$, $k=5$, and $k=10$. We hypothesize that this occurs because HotpotQA distractor paragraphs are deliberately semantically similar, so naive similarity-gap truncation cannot reliably isolate the true supporting evidence.

These findings motivate CART (Cost-Penalized Adaptive Routing for Test-Time Retrieval), a training-free controller that decides whether to retrieve, when retrieval is worth its token cost, and what context to keep. CART combines adaptive-$k$ selection via similarity gaps~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, a UCB-Cost routing policy extending UCB1~\cite{auer2002ucb1}, and a noise-filtering gate. Our goal is to approach the answer quality of strong fixed-$k$ baselines, especially $k=10$, while using substantially fewer tokens. Code is available at \url{https://github.com/arnml/cart-code}.

\keywords{Retrieval-Augmented Generation \and Test-time Compute \and Adaptive Routing \and Bandits \and Token Cost}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a standard approach for improving large language model (LLM) performance on knowledge-intensive tasks~\cite{lewis2020rag}. In many deployed and benchmarked RAG systems, retrieval is still implemented as a fixed top-$k$ policy that appends the same number of documents to every query~\cite{klesel2025rag,zhao2026aigcsurvey,yu2024rankrag,lee2025setr,verma2026reflectiverag}. This design is straightforward, but it assumes that all queries benefit from the same retrieval budget. In practice, that assumption is often too rigid: some queries can be answered from parametric knowledge alone, some require external evidence, and increasing $k$ increases the token budget substantially.

This issue becomes sharper on multi-hop question answering. We evaluate on HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, a benchmark designed to require reasoning across multiple pieces of evidence. In the distractor setting used here, each example contains exactly 10 paragraphs: 2 contain the supporting facts and 8 are distractors. This makes the dataset especially useful for studying both retrieval utility and retrieval difficulty. In our updated baselines, average F1 rises steadily as more retrieved paragraphs are provided. That upward trend is visible in the F1-vs.-$k$ plot and suggests that, for this dataset and these models, larger context budgets are often usable. A plausible reason is that the paragraphs are short relative to modern context windows. However, the systems cost is still substantial: for GPT-5.4-mini, mean total tokens increase from 103.5 in \texttt{always\_think} to 1452.5 in \texttt{retrieval\_k10}, roughly a 14$\times$ increase.

We take fixed $k=10$ as a strong quality upper baseline and ask whether a smarter controller can recover similar answer quality with far fewer tokens. A natural candidate is Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, which selects a query-specific cutoff from similarity-score gaps. Intuitively, this should be an efficient way to keep only the most relevant paragraphs. Yet our results show that Adaptive-$k$ alone does not solve the problem in this benchmark. On GPT-5.4-mini it selects a compact mean $k^*=3.02$, but with F1 0.627 it underperforms even fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k3} (0.651). This suggests that the hard part is not only \emph{how many} paragraphs to keep, but also \emph{whether} retrieval is needed at all and \emph{which} retrieved paragraphs are genuinely useful when distractors are semantically similar.

We address this problem with CART (\textbf{C}ost-\textbf{P}enalized \textbf{A}daptive \textbf{R}outing for \textbf{T}est-time Retrieval), a training-free controller that decides, at inference time, whether to retrieve and how much context to pass to the model. CART combines three ideas: (i) adaptive-$k$ context selection based on inter-document similarity gaps~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, (ii) a UCB-Cost routing policy that extends UCB1~\cite{auer2002ucb1} with an explicit token-cost penalty, and (iii) a noise-filtering gate that suppresses low-value or redundant context before generation.

Our experiments focus primarily on OpenAI models, where comparisons are cleaner and provider-specific confounds are reduced. Anthropic models are included as auxiliary reference baselines. Across these baselines, we observe three consistent patterns: retrieval is useful, fixed $k=10$ is a strong but expensive baseline, and Adaptive-$k$ alone does not yet match the strongest fixed baselines. Together, these patterns motivate adaptive, cost-aware, and noise-aware retrieval control at inference time.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate retrieval control as a training-free inference-time routing problem under a quality--cost tradeoff.
    \item We provide updated baseline evidence on HotpotQA showing that fixed $k=10$ is a strong quality baseline but requires roughly 12$\times$--15$\times$ more tokens than no retrieval.
    \item We provide a partial Adaptive-$k$ ablation showing that similarity-gap truncation alone does not match the strongest fixed baselines in this distractor setting.
    \item We introduce CART as a controller for deciding whether to retrieve, when retrieval is worth the added token cost, and what retrieved context to preserve.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Fixed-Budget Retrieval in RAG}

Foundational RAG systems use a fixed top-$k$ retriever~\cite{lewis2020rag}, and recent journal treatments still describe plain or vanilla RAG in exactly these terms. Klesel and Wittmann~\cite{klesel2025rag} summarize plain RAG as retrieving the most relevant chunks or documents, most commonly by selecting the top $k$ hits. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2026aigcsurvey} likewise review RAG as top-$k$ similarity retrieval followed by augmentation and generation. Recent peer-reviewed RAG papers continue to position fixed top-$k$ as the default point of comparison: RankRAG~\cite{yu2024rankrag} notes that LLMs typically use top-$k$ contexts from a retriever, SetR~\cite{lee2025setr} critiques existing approaches that rerank top-$k$ passages, and ReflectiveRAG~\cite{verma2026reflectiverag} explicitly contrasts its method with current fixed top-$k$ retrieval pipelines.

\subsection{Adaptive Retrieval with Training}

Several methods adapt retrieval behavior through training. Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag} learns retrieval control through reflection tokens. DRAGIN~\cite{su2024dragin} dynamically triggers retrieval based on real-time information needs. DioR~\cite{guo2025dior}, RetrievalQA~\cite{zhang2024retrievalqa}, and RA-RAG~\cite{hwang2025rarag} all explore learned or additional retrieval adaptation mechanisms. These approaches demonstrate the value of adaptive retrieval, but they require additional training, specialized learned components, or both.

\subsection{Training-Free Adaptive Context Selection}

Among training-free methods, Adaptive-k~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} is most closely related to our setting. It selects the number of retrieved documents on a per-query basis using similarity-score gaps, showing that useful context can often be compact. However, Adaptive-k assumes that retrieval has already been invoked. Our problem is broader: we study whether to retrieve, when retrieval is worth its cost, and how much context to retain under an explicit token-budget objective.

\subsection{Retrieval, Reasoning, and Test-Time Compute}

Other work studies tighter integration between retrieval and reasoning. IRCoT interleaves retrieval with chain-of-thought reasoning for multi-step QA~\cite{trivedi2023interleave}, while ReAct couples reasoning traces with actions such as search or environment interaction~\cite{yao2023react}. In parallel, a growing literature on adaptive test-time compute studies how much reasoning compute to allocate at inference time~\cite{snell2025ttc}. CART is complementary to this line of work: rather than controlling reasoning depth alone, it controls evidence acquisition cost.

\subsection{Benchmark Validity and Contamination}

Open-domain QA benchmarks may overlap with model training data~\cite{li2024c2leva,yi2026membership}. We therefore avoid interpreting stronger no-retrieval performance as definitive evidence that a model robustly ``knows'' the answer. Broader pretraining exposure or contamination may contribute to parametric-only performance. However, our main empirical point does not depend on distinguishing these causes: even newer models with stronger no-retrieval baselines continue to benefit from retrieval on HotpotQA.

\section{CART: Training-Free Test-Time Controller}

\subsection{Problem Setup}

Given a query $q$, a retrieval pool $D$, and a language model $m$, our goal is to choose an inference strategy that balances answer quality and token cost. We consider two high-level actions:
\[
A = \{\texttt{think}, \texttt{retrieve}\}.
\]
The \texttt{think} action answers directly from parametric knowledge. The \texttt{retrieve} action first retrieves candidate documents, then answers using filtered context.

\subsection{Adaptive-$K$ Selection}

CART begins with a broad retrieval step that returns the top-$N$ candidates for a query, where $N=10$ in our current setup. Let the similarity scores of the retrieved documents be
\[
s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \cdots \ge s_N.
\]
Following Adaptive-k~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, CART selects a query-specific cut point using the largest adjacent gap:
\begin{equation}
k^* = \arg\max_{1 \le i < N} \left(s_i - s_{i+1}\right).
\end{equation}
Intuitively, this selects the compact cluster of highly relevant documents and avoids blindly passing the full retrieved list to the generator. In the current benchmark, however, distractor paragraphs are often semantically similar enough that similarity-gap selection alone may not recover the true supporting evidence, which is exactly why Adaptive-$k$ is only one component of CART rather than the entire method.

\subsection{UCB-Cost Policy}

CART treats routing as a lightweight bandit problem. For each action $a \in A$, we maintain an estimated utility $\hat{Q}(a)$ and a visit count $n_a$. We then score actions using
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(a) = \hat{Q}(a) + \beta \sqrt{\frac{\ln N_t}{n_a + 1}} - \lambda \, c(a),
\end{equation}
where $N_t$ is the number of decisions made so far, $c(a)$ is the normalized cost of the action, $\beta$ controls exploration, and $\lambda$ controls cost sensitivity. The key addition relative to standard UCB1~\cite{auer2002ucb1} is the explicit cost penalty term $-\lambda c(a)$, which biases the controller toward cheaper actions when expected quality is similar.

In our setting, \texttt{think} is cheaper than \texttt{retrieve}, but retrieval may yield better answer quality. The policy therefore learns to prefer retrieval when the expected gain justifies the added token cost.

\subsection{Noise Gate}

After adaptive-$k$ selection, CART applies a simple noise gate before generation. The gate removes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item documents whose query similarity is below a minimum threshold, and
    \item documents that are highly redundant with already selected context.
\end{enumerate}
This component is motivated directly by the HotpotQA distractor setting. Because 8 of the 10 context paragraphs are intentionally irrelevant yet often still topically related, passing more retrieved text is not guaranteed to provide only useful evidence. The noise gate is meant to preserve high-value context while reducing distractor spillover into the generation stage.

\subsection{Pseudocode}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:cart} summarizes the full procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{CART inference}
\label{alg:cart}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Query $q$, model $m$, retrieval pool $D$, pool size $N$
\State Retrieve top-$N$ candidate documents $C = \{d_1,\dots,d_N\}$ for $q$
\State Compute similarity scores $s_1 \ge \cdots \ge s_N$
\State Select $k^*$ using the largest-gap rule
\State Keep top-$k^*$ documents: $R \leftarrow \{d_1,\dots,d_{k^*}\}$
\State Apply noise filtering to $R$
\State Compute $\mathrm{score}(\texttt{think})$ and $\mathrm{score}(\texttt{retrieve})$
\If{$\mathrm{score}(\texttt{retrieve}) > \mathrm{score}(\texttt{think})$ and $R \neq \emptyset$}
    \State Answer using retrieved context $R$
    \State $a \leftarrow \texttt{retrieve}$
\Else
    \State Answer directly from parametric knowledge
    \State $a \leftarrow \texttt{think}$
\EndIf
\State Update action statistics for $a$
\State \Return answer, chosen action, selected context size
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{EM}: exact match
    \item \textbf{F1}: token-level F1
    \item \textbf{Input / Output / Total Tokens}: average token usage per example
    \item \textbf{Cost}: average cost per example in USD
\end{itemize}

Our main paper emphasis is on F1, token usage, and cost. We no longer report derived token-efficiency or cost-efficiency metrics, since the central practical question is whether CART can approach strong fixed-baseline quality with a substantially smaller token budget.

\subsection{Baselines}

We compare CART against four fixed policies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{always\_think}: no retrieval, answer directly
    \item \textbf{retrieval\_k3}: retrieve top 3 paragraphs
    \item \textbf{retrieval\_k5}: retrieve top 5 paragraphs
    \item \textbf{retrieval\_k10}: retrieve top 10 paragraphs
\end{itemize}

These baselines are meant to answer two questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does retrieval help relative to parametric-only answering?
    \item If fixed $k=10$ is a strong quality baseline, how much token cost does it impose relative to cheaper strategies?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implementation Details}

Our main experiments focus on two OpenAI models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{gpt-4o-mini}
    \item \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}
\end{itemize}
We use these as the primary setting for CART because within-provider comparisons reduce confounds related to tokenization, pricing, and provider-specific prompting behavior.

For the updated OpenAI baselines, we evaluate a random sample of $n=400$ HotpotQA distractor validation examples. The \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} retrieval runs completed on 399 examples, while the remaining OpenAI runs completed on the full 400; all table values are averages over completed examples. As auxiliary reference baselines, we also report Claude Haiku 4.5 on $n=400$ examples and Claude Sonnet 4.6 on $n=50$ examples. We retain the smaller Sonnet run as a preliminary reference point and treat it more cautiously than the larger OpenAI and Haiku runs.

For the fixed baselines, retrieval is performed over the 10 paragraphs provided by each HotpotQA distractor example. CART uses the same retrieval pool and differs only in how it selects context and routes between actions. We also report a partial Adaptive-$k$ evaluation on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} over $n=400$ examples, isolating the context-selection component before adding routing and noise filtering.

\subsection{Cost--Performance Tradeoff}

Across all four models, moving from \texttt{always\_think} to retrieval produces large gains in EM and F1. On this benchmark, the average quality trend with larger $k$ is upward rather than downward. However, those gains are bought with a dramatic increase in token usage. For example, GPT-5.4-mini improves from F1 0.387 to 0.792 as mean total tokens increase from 103.5 to 1452.5, and Claude Haiku 4.5 improves from 0.367 to 0.779 as tokens increase from 132.7 to 1639.8. The key systems question is therefore: can we retain most of the quality of fixed $k=10$ without paying the full token cost of always passing all ten paragraphs?

Cost is still useful to report because it is easy to read and compare, but token usage is the optimization target in this work. The per-example cost remains small in absolute terms, yet the underlying token budgets scale sharply with larger $k$. For deployment-oriented systems, that token growth matters for throughput, latency, and total spend even when the individual dollar amounts remain modest.

\subsection{Adaptivity Across Queries}

The updated results suggest a shift in emphasis. Average performance no longer argues strongly for a smaller global fixed $k$; instead, it shows that fixed $k=10$ is a strong upper baseline. But a global $k=10$ policy still spends the full retrieval budget on every example, including questions that may be answerable without retrieval or with much less context. This is where adaptivity remains important. The problem is not to find one universally best small $k$, but to allocate retrieval only when it is useful and to keep only the parts of retrieved context that actually contribute to answering.

This is also why query-level control matters more than model-level averages. Even if $k=10$ wins on average, that does not imply every query needs ten paragraphs. CART is intended to learn exactly this distinction: whether to retrieve, when retrieval is worth the extra budget, and what subset of retrieved context should survive to generation.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

We currently have a partial ablation for Adaptive-$k$ alone on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}. Table~\ref{tab:adaptivek_partial} shows that the method selects compact contexts, with mean $k^*=3.02$ and median $k^*=2$. On its face, this seems promising: it uses 483.1 mean total tokens, far below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k10} at 1452.5. However, its F1 is only 0.627, which is below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k3} (0.651), \texttt{retrieval\_k5} (0.716), and \texttt{retrieval\_k10} (0.792). In other words, naive Adaptive-$k$ is efficient in token count but not yet competitive in answer quality.

This result is important for the paper. Adaptive-$k$ looks like the natural answer to the token problem, but in this benchmark it is not enough. A plausible explanation is that the distractor paragraphs are deliberately built to sound relevant, so simple embedding similarity and gap-based truncation do not reliably identify the true supporting evidence. This is exactly the gap CART is designed to address by combining context selection with routing and noise filtering.

\section{Discussion}

Our updated baselines support three practical conclusions. First, retrieval remains a major lever for multi-hop QA performance. Across all tested models, moving from \texttt{always\_think} to \texttt{retrieval\_k10} yields a large F1 gain.

Second, the current HotpotQA distractor results suggest that average performance often improves with more retrieved context. This likely reflects a combination of short paragraph lengths and modern long-context models. We therefore should not frame the benchmark as showing that larger $k$ is broadly harmful. Instead, we should frame it as showing that fixed $k=10$ is a strong but expensive baseline.

Third, the partial Adaptive-$k$ ablation shows why the problem is not solved by naive semantic truncation. Although Adaptive-$k$ chooses compact contexts, it loses too much answer quality in the current setting. This suggests that semantically similar distractors make ``retrieve the top few by embedding signal'' an incomplete solution. In other words, the hard problem is not just how many paragraphs to keep, but whether retrieval is needed at all, when it is worth paying for, and what retrieved evidence is actually useful.

We deliberately focus CART on OpenAI models in the main experiments. This design choice keeps the central analysis cleaner and reduces cross-provider confounds such as pricing, tokenization, and provider-specific prompting behavior. Anthropic results remain useful as auxiliary evidence that the broader retrieval pattern is not unique to one provider.

We note several limitations. HotpotQA is not contamination-free, so retrieval gains should not be interpreted as a clean decomposition between knowledge and memorization. Our current results are also limited to single-turn QA, fixed baseline policies, and a partial Adaptive-$k$ ablation rather than the full CART controller. Future work should test whether the same patterns hold in multi-turn agent settings and on contamination-resistant benchmarks.

\section{Conclusion}

Our updated HotpotQA distractor baselines show that fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k10} is a strong quality baseline across all tested models, but it requires roughly 12$\times$--15$\times$ more tokens than no retrieval. A partial Adaptive-$k$ evaluation on GPT-5.4-mini selects compact contexts and uses far fewer tokens than $k=10$, but it does not match the quality of the strongest fixed baselines. This motivates the full CART design: not just adaptive truncation, but a controller for deciding whether to retrieve, when retrieval is worth the token cost, and what retrieved context should be preserved. The next step is to test whether the full controller can approach the quality of fixed $k=10$ while operating with a substantially smaller token budget.
