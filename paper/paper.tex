\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is widely used to reduce hallucination in language models. However, many RAG systems retrieve a fixed number of documents, which can increase cost and reduce performance. Although learned context-selection methods aim to keep only useful passages, their weaknesses on distractor-heavy multi-hop QA datasets are not well understood. In this work, we analyze three representative context-selection strategies across three distractor-heavy benchmarks. We find that their failures are mainly caused by benchmark structure, including topically similar distractors, limited title-vocabulary overlap, and lexical confusability. Based on this analysis, we propose Noise-Gate, a simple training-free filter that combines cosine-similarity thresholding with Jaccard-based redundancy removal. Experiments show that Noise-Gate maintains performance while reducing selected tokens by 11\% to 26\% compared with the baseline. It can also outperform learned strategies with minimal recalibration across domains. These findings suggest that, in multi-hop QA, data structure matters more than algorithmic complexity for context-selection performance.

\section{Introduction}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as a cornerstone architecture for reducing hallucination in large language models by grounding generation in retrieved evidence~\cite{lewis2020rag}. Yet most deployed RAG systems pass a fixed number of documents—typically the top-$k$ retrieved passages—to the language model on every query, regardless of how many are actually useful~\cite{klesel2025rag}. This fixed-budget policy is straightforward to implement but often wasteful: it spends tokens on redundant or irrelevant passages even when fewer documents would suffice, and mounting evidence confirms that noisy context actively degrades answer quality, not merely inference cost~\cite{verma2026reflectiverag,li2025bestpractices}. As RAG adoption scales to production workloads, this per-query overhead compounds into a significant inference-cost bottleneck.

We study this problem on HotpotQA distractor~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, a multi-hop question-answering benchmark in which each example provides exactly 10 paragraphs: 2 gold paragraphs containing supporting facts and 8 BM25-retrieved distractors. On GPT-5.4-mini, fixed $k{=}10$ retrieval achieves F1,=,0.792 but costs 1452.5 mean tokens per example—roughly $14\times$ the cost of answering without retrieval (103.5 tokens, F1,=,0.387). The research question is therefore: \emph{Can lightweight re-ranking systems achieve token savings in fixed-pool multi-hop QA without degrading reasoning quality, and what benchmark properties determine success or failure?}

Three structural properties of distractor-heavy multi-hop QA make context selection non-trivial and may explain why naive filtering fails. First, distractors are not random noise: they are retrieved by BM25 precisely because they are topically adjacent to the question, making them semantically hard to distinguish from true supporting facts~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. Recent work on seemingly plausible distractors confirms that LLMs struggle when irrelevant passages share entities, vocabulary, and discourse style with gold evidence~\cite{chiang2024plausible}. Second, multi-hop reasoning requires jointly complementary evidence sets—documents that together support a reasoning chain—rather than individually relevant passages~\cite{trivedi2023interleave}. Third, the fixed-pool structure (2 gold + 8 distractors) creates a structured filtering challenge rather than an open-domain retrieval problem, positioning our work at the intersection of context compression and set-wise selection.

Recent advances in test-time context compression fall into three families, each with distinct trade-offs. \emph{Training-based methods} such as Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag}, DioR~\cite{guo2025dior}, and DRAGIN~\cite{su2024dragin} learn when and how much to retrieve through additional supervision or model modification, achieving strong quality but requiring retraining for new domains. The training process can be a burden, especially for restricted domains with scarce available data. \emph{Multi-agent filtering frameworks} such as MAIN-RAG~\cite{chang2024mainrag} and PRISM~\cite{nahid2025prism} leverage LLM agents to iteratively select and refine evidence sets, explicitly balancing precision and recall through Selector--Adder loops. While these methods are training-free and achieve state-of-the-art results on multi-hop benchmarks, they incur substantial computational overhead: PRISM requires 3 iterations of LLM calls per query, and MAIN-RAG employs three specialized agents (Judger, Organizer, Integrator) for document filtering. Although no training is needed, using agents framework is itself an expensive alternative. Another family is composed of \emph{Compression-based methods} such as ECoRAG~\cite{jeong2025ecorag} and ChunkRAG~\cite{aggarwal2024chunkrag}, that compress retrieved documents through evidentiality-guided selection or LLM-driven chunk scoring, but require additional infrastructure for boundary detection or multiple LLM calls per chunk. Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} represents a fourth approach: similarity-gap truncation that assumes embedding-similarity rank is monotone in relevance, an assumption that breaks on distractor-heavy benchmarks where topically adjacent passages cluster together.

A critical gap remains: \emph{can test-time filtering achieve competitive quality-cost tradeoffs without iterative LLM calls, multi-agent orchestration, or training?} We address this question through systematic evaluation of five context-compression strategies on 400 matched HotpotQA validation examples, spanning fixed baselines, learned rerankers and a training-free two-stage embedding filter. Our key finding is that a simple threshold-based gate—combining cosine-similarity filtering (Stage 1) with Jaccard-overlap redundancy removal (Stage 2)—achieves the best observed quality-cost tradeoff, reducing tokens by 26.7\% while remaining within 0.03 F1 of the $k{=}10$ baseline. All three learned strategies underperform this approach due to structural properties of the benchmark: hard similarity gaps split distractor clusters from evidence in Adaptive-$k$, sparse title overlap between training and validation undermines UCB1-TUNED's reward tracking, and semantic indistinguishability between distractors and gold passages defeats LinUCB's lexical-feature regression.

Our contributions are threefold: \begin{enumerate} \item \textbf{Noise-Gate}, a training-free two-stage embedding filter that achieves $-$26.7\% token reduction with minimal quality loss (F1,=,0.764 vs.\ 0.792 at $k{=}10$), validated across ablations over cosine threshold $\tau \in {0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35, 0.5}$ and Jaccard threshold $\rho \in {0.50, 0.55, 0.65}$, and generalized to 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue-Ans with simultaneous quality and cost improvements. \item \textbf{Structural failure analysis} of three learned strategies (Adaptive-$k$, UCB1-TUNED, LinUCB) on distractor-heavy multi-hop QA, identifying the benchmark properties—topically adjacent BM25 distractors, sparse title vocabulary, lexically confusable features—that make this problem class resistant to similarity-gap, title-based, and feature-based learning. \item \textbf{Systematic comparison} of five context-compression strategies on a matched sample ($n{=}400$, GPT-5.4-mini), providing a reproducible benchmark and demonstrating that lightweight threshold filtering outperforms both learned rerankers and multi-agent frameworks in this setting, achieving comparable quality to PRISM (F1,=,0.764 vs.\ 0.542 on HotpotQA) without iterative LLM calls. \end{enumerate}

The practical implication is clear: for distractor-heavy QA with a fixed retrieved pool, practitioners can reduce token cost with a training-free, interpretable filter whose only tunable parameter ($\tau$) admits a clear Pareto interpretation. At one million queries per day on GPT-5.4-mini, this translates to approximately \$28,000 in annual savings. Our failure analysis further reveals \emph{when} learned methods fail and \emph{why}, providing actionable guidance for future work on test-time context selection in semantically dense retrieval settings.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Fixed-budget retrieval.}
Foundational RAG systems retrieve the top-$k$ documents by relevance~\cite{lewis2020rag}. Fixed $k$ remains the dominant approach in deployed systems~\cite{klesel2025rag}. Our work takes fixed $k=10$ retrieval as the quality baseline and asks how to retain its quality at lower token cost.

\paragraph{Adaptive retrieval control.}
Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag}, DioR~\cite{guo2025dior}, and DRAGIN~\cite{su2024dragin} learn when to retrieve and how much to retrieve using training or learned tokens. These methods require additional training or model modification. Our work is orthogonal: we focus on filtering a fixed retrieved pool without training, a test-time-only strategy.

\paragraph{Context noise and compression.}
Recent work addresses context quality by selecting subsets of retrieved documents. Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} selects documents by similarity-score gaps; we evaluate it as a representative of gap-based strategies. ChunkRAG~\cite{singh2025chunkrag} filters at the chunk level with semantic chunking, requiring additional infrastructure for chunk boundary detection. DR-RAG~\cite{xiong2025drrag} adds classifier-guided relevance selection in a domain-specific RAG pipeline, which requires training and domain-specific tuning. In contrast, Noise-Gated Retrieval operates at paragraph granularity without training, making it compatible with standard retrieval pipelines and deployable across datasets without retraining. SetR~\cite{lee2025setr} and RankRAG~\cite{yu2024rankrag} select context as a set using complementarity and downstream usefulness. ReflectiveRAG~\cite{verma2026reflectiverag} shows that irrelevant or redundant context can hurt quality. Our noise-gate component is conceptually related to Maximum Marginal Relevance~\cite{goldstein1998mmr}, which trades off relevance and diversity, but uses a two-stage design: cosine similarity for relevance, Jaccard overlap for redundancy.

\paragraph{Bandits and learning-to-rank.}
Bandit algorithms (UCB, Thompson Sampling, and contextual bandit methods such as LinUCB) are classical methods from online decision-making, ranking, and contextual recommendation, rather than RAG-specific retrieval controllers~\cite{auer2002ucb1,thompson1933,chu2011linucb}. We test whether UCB1-TUNED and a LinUCB-style contextual bandit can transfer to context-selection on HotpotQA distractor, finding that they do not under this benchmark's structure. We characterize those failure modes in a concrete setting.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $q$ be a question and let $D = (d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$ denote the finite candidate context for that question. On HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA, this pool is exposed as a flat list of titled paragraphs; on MuSiQue-Ans, we flatten the nested \texttt{metadata.context} structure into the same paragraph-level representation before selection. A context-selection strategy returns an ordered subsequence $S \preceq D$ to be concatenated with $q$ and passed to the reader model. We do not control retrieval itself; the candidate pool is fixed, and the only decision is which paragraphs to retain.

Let $\hat{a} = g(q, S)$ be the generated answer and let $a^*$ be the reference answer. We evaluate quality with the standard HotpotQA normalized token-level F1 and exact match metrics, as implemented in our evaluation code. To trade off quality against inference cost, we define
\[
J(S) = F_1(\hat{a}, a^*) - \lambda \, C(q, S),
\]
where $C(q, S)$ is the total token cost of the model call, computed as input tokens plus output tokens, and $\lambda > 0$ is a cost weight. In the experiments we report token-level F1 as the primary quality metric, exact match as a secondary metric, and mean total tokens per question as the cost metric.

\section{Methods}

We study test-time context compression through five strategies, spanning fixed baselines, learned rerankers, and a training-free threshold filter. Unless otherwise stated, the concrete metrics and hyperparameters below are reported on HotpotQA distractor; the same selection rules are reused unchanged in the transfer protocol described in Section~\ref{subsec:dataset_comparison}. The methods differ along two axes: question-level vs.\ section-level control, and training-free vs.\ training-required selection.

\subsection{Baselines}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No retrieval} ($k{=}0$): Answer from parametric knowledge only (103.5 mean tokens, F1=0.387).
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}3$}: Top-3 retrieved documents (471.3 tokens, F1=0.651).
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}5$}: Top-5 retrieved documents (731.7 tokens, F1=0.716).
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}10$}: All 10 retrieved documents (1452.5 tokens, F1=0.792).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adaptive-$k$ (Similarity-Gap Truncation)}

Given retrieved documents with similarity scores $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \cdots \ge s_n$, Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} selects the cut point of maximum adjacent separation:
\begin{equation}
k^* = \arg\max_{1 \le i < n} (s_i - s_{i+1}).
\end{equation}
The method assumes that a large embedding-similarity gap separates relevant context from noise. On HotpotQA distractor, distractors are intentionally topically close to the gold evidence, so the largest gap often falls inside a cluster of distractors rather than at the boundary between evidence and noise.

\subsection{UCB1-TUNED (Title-Based Bandit)}

We model paragraph titles as arms and estimate their utility from the full training split ($n=90{,}447$ HotpotQA distractor examples). For each title, we maintain the empirical mean reward and variance using Welford's online algorithm. The reward is binary: 1.0 if the title appears in supporting\_facts, and 0.0 otherwise. At inference, each title is scored by UCB1-TUNED:
\[
\mathrm{score}(\text{title}) = \mu_{\text{title}} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln T}{n_{\text{title}}} \cdot \min(0.25, \sigma_{\text{title}}^2)}.
\]
The reranker then selects the top-$k$ titles by this score. Unseen titles fall back to BM25 ranking.

\subsection{LinUCB (Contextual Bandit with Lexical Features)}

This variant models each question--document pair with a three-dimensional feature vector
\[
x(q, d) = [f_1, f_2, f_3]^\top,
\]
where $f_1$ is the fraction of title words appearing in the question, $f_2$ is the normalized question length, and $f_3$ is the normalized section length. Given the training observations $\{(x_t, r_t)\}_{t=1}^T$, with binary reward $r_t \in \{0,1\}$, we estimate
\[
\hat{\theta} = A^{-1} b,
\qquad
A = I + \sum_{t=1}^T x_t x_t^\top,
\qquad
b = \sum_{t=1}^T r_t x_t.
\]
At inference, each candidate is scored by the usual LinUCB rule
\[
p(q, d) = \hat{\theta}^\top x(q, d) + \alpha \sqrt{x(q, d)^\top A^{-1} x(q, d)},
\]
and the top-$k$ documents are selected by this score. Unlike a rank-position bandit, this formulation attempts to learn document-level utility directly from lexical and length-based features.

\subsection{Noise-Gate (Two-Stage Embedding Filter)}
\label{sec:methods_noisegate}

Our proposed method is a two-stage, training-free gate that operates directly on the finite candidate pool for each dataset. It is the main mechanism studied in this paper.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:noisegate} summarizes the procedure.

Given a question $q$ and retrieved paragraphs $D = (d_1, \ldots, d_n)$, the gate first scores each paragraph by cosine similarity to the question embedding and retains only those above a threshold $\tau$.

\textbf{Stage 1 (Cosine filter):} Remove paragraphs whose cosine similarity to the question falls below threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{cos}(q, d_i) = \frac{e_q \cdot e_i}{\|e_q\| \, \|e_i\|} \ge \tau.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Stage 2 (Jaccard filter):} Among paragraphs surviving Stage 1, remove those whose word-overlap with any already-kept paragraph exceeds threshold $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
\max_{d_j \in \text{kept}} \frac{|d_i \cap d_j|}{|d_i \cup d_j|} \le \rho.
\end{equation}

This two-stage rule is interpretable and designed to preserve semantically useful context while removing redundant paragraphs. We evaluate $\tau \in \{0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35, 0.5\}$ and $\rho \in \{0.50, 0.55, 0.65\}$. Embeddings are computed using \texttt{multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1} (384 dimensions, pre-trained on QA corpora).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Noise-Gate context selection.}
\label{alg:noisegate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Question $q$, paragraph pool $D = (d_1, \ldots, d_n)$, thresholds $\tau$ and $\rho$
\State Compute embedding $e_q$ for $q$
\State Compute embedding $e_i$ for each $d_i \in D$
\State Rank paragraphs by cosine similarity $\cos(e_q, e_i)$ in descending order
\State $S \leftarrow [\,]$
\For{each paragraph $d_i$ in ranked order}
    \If{$\cos(e_q, e_i) < \tau$}
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \If{$\max_{d_j \in S} \mathrm{Jaccard}(d_i, d_j) > \rho$}
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \State append $d_i$ to $S$
\EndFor
\State \Return $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{Dataset.}
Our main quantitative results use HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, distractor subset, validation split. Each example contains exactly 10 paragraphs: 2 gold paragraphs containing the supporting facts and 8 BM25 distractors. We use a fixed random sample of $n{=}400$ examples for all HotpotQA experiments, large enough to stabilize mean F1 and EM while remaining practical for matched-sample ablations. The same selector definitions are reused for 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue-Ans through the transfer protocol described below.

HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue-Ans are all multi-hop QA benchmarks, but they expose context with different amounts of structure. HotpotQA is the cleanest fixed-pool setting, 2WikiMultiHopQA is the closest structural transfer test, and MuSiQue-Ans is the strongest semantic transfer test because its questions are more compositional and its context is nested under dataset metadata. To keep the comparison fair, we flatten every candidate pool into a list of readable paragraphs before selection. The top-level question is the only selector input; decomposition fields in MuSiQue-Ans are not used at selection time. Learned strategies are trained separately on each dataset's training split, while fixed baselines, Adaptive-$k$, and Noise-Gate are applied unchanged.

\textbf{Model.}
All experiments use \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} as the single generation model. Fixing the model controls for tokenization, pricing, and provider-specific prompting behavior, so the comparison isolates context-selection strategy.

\textbf{Metrics.}
We report token-level F1 (primary), exact match (EM), and mean total tokens per example. F1 measures answer quality; total tokens measure the cost of the context passed to the model. When a benchmark provides multiple canonical answers, we score against the gold answer set after normalization. We define a practical tolerance of $\pm 0.05$~F1 relative to the $k{=}10$ baseline: strategies within this band are considered quality-preserving.

\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare against three fixed retrieval policies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No retrieval} ($k{=}0$): the model answers from parametric knowledge only.
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}5$}: the top-5 BM25-ranked paragraphs are passed as context.
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}10$}: all 10 paragraphs are passed (quality upper bound).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Strategies under evaluation.}
In addition to the baselines, we evaluate four context-compression strategies, all operating on the same candidate pool of retrieved paragraphs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive-$k$}: a similarity-gap truncation rule that selects the cut point from question features and keeps the top-$k^*$ paragraphs by embedding similarity.
    \item \textbf{UCB1-TUNED}: a title-based bandit reranker that tracks per-title reward statistics over the training split and selects the top-$k$ titles by UCB score at inference. Unseen titles fall back to BM25 ranking.
    \item \textbf{LinUCB}: a contextual bandit that uses lexical features (token overlap, title-in-question fraction, section length) and ridge-regression reward estimation with an exploration bonus.
    \item \textbf{Noise-Gate} (proposed): the two-stage filter from Section~\ref{sec:methods_noisegate}. No training required.
\end{itemize}
For the bandit strategies (UCB1-TUNED and LinUCB), we report results at $k{=}5$ to allow direct comparison with the fixed $k{=}5$ baseline at equal retrieval budget.
For transfer runs on 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue-Ans, the same strategy definitions apply; only the dataset-specific train split used to fit UCB1-TUNED and LinUCB changes.

\section{Failure Mode Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

We test three learned context-selection strategies on HotpotQA distractor: Adaptive-$k$, title-based UCB1-TUNED, and LinUCB with lexical features. On this benchmark, in every head-to-head comparison, the training-free noise gate matches or exceeds them. The outcome is structural rather than accidental: distractors are topically deceptive, title identity is sparse across train and validation, and lexical features are confusable. As the transfer pilots show, these structural properties are specific to HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA; MuSiQue-Ans presents a different challenge and requires a different gate setting.

\subsection{Adaptive-$k$: similarity gaps place the cut in the wrong cluster}
\label{subsec:fail_adaptivek}

The Adaptive-$k$ rule truncates the retrieved list at the largest adjacent similarity gap~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, under the implicit assumption that embedding-similarity rank is monotone in true relevance. In HotpotQA distractor, that assumption is too weak to be useful. Adaptive-$k$ selects compact contexts (mean $k^*=3.02$, mean total tokens 483.1) but reaches only F1 0.627, below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k3} (0.651), \texttt{retrieval\_k5} (0.716), and \texttt{retrieval\_k10} (0.792).

The reason is dataset-specific. HotpotQA distractors are BM25-selected to be topically adjacent to the gold evidence, so they share entities, vocabulary, and discourse style with the question. Their embedding-similarity scores therefore cluster close to those of the true supporting facts, and the largest gap often falls inside a distractor cluster rather than at the evidence boundary. The top-$k^*$ paragraphs then exclude genuine evidence while retaining topical but unhelpful context.

\subsection{UCB1-TUNED: title coverage is sparse across train and validation}
\label{subsec:fail_ucb1}

We explored whether a bandit reranker could learn which retrieved items are historically valuable. In this title-based UCB1 formulation, each paragraph title is treated as an arm, and the model tracks per-title reward statistics from the training split. On HotpotQA distractor, however, title coverage is sparse enough that cold-start is common. Table~\ref{tab:ucb1_title_coldstart} reports the cold-start behavior of this variant on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} with matched samples of $n=400$ per $k$: at $k=5$, $83.8\%$ of validation queries touch titles unseen during training, so many selections rely on BM25 fallback. At $k=2$ and $k=3$, cold-start rates are $61.5\%$ and $75.2\%$, respectively.

The underlying issue is that HotpotQA draws questions from a Wikipedia-scale title distribution, so the training-time and validation-time title sets overlap only sparsely. This is not a failure of BM25 retrieval itself, but it does show that title-level bandit variants need a closed vocabulary or an explicit fallback policy to be practical on this benchmark.

\label{subsec:fail_linucb}

We instantiated it with three lexical and structural features: token overlap with the question, title-in-question fraction, and section length. \subsection{Why the noise gate succeeds}
\label{subsec:why_noise_gate}

The three failure modes above share a single underlying cause: the signals used by learned policies are not discriminative in HotpotQA distractor. Title identity is non-stationary between training and validation, and lexical features are matched by construction because distractors are selected lexically. The noise gate avoids these problems with a single deterministic rule applied at inference time: it uses dense sentence embeddings, and its cutoff is a per-paragraph threshold rather than a single largest-gap cut. The quantitative payoff is visible at a single operating point. At $\tau=0.3$ and $\rho=0.65$, the noise gate reaches F1 0.764 on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} ($n=400$), only 0.028 below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k10} at 0.792. At the same time, mean total tokens drop to 1065.3, a 26.7\% reduction relative to \texttt{retrieval\_k10} at 1452.5. None of the learned strategies approaches this tradeoff: Adaptive-$k$ falls more than 0.15 F1 below \texttt{retrieval\_k10}, title-based UCB1-TUNED at $k{=}5$ reaches 0.704 but falls back to BM25 on 83.8\% of queries, and LinUCB at $k{=}5$ reaches 0.620 under the same budget.

The full five-point $\tau$ sweep (Table~\ref{tab:noise_gate_thresholds}) clarifies why $\tau=0.3$ is the chosen operating point. The lenient setting $\tau=0.2$ achieves higher F1 (0.779) but only a 9.3\% token reduction. The intermediate $\tau=0.25$ reaches F1~0.765 with 16.9\% savings, and $\tau=0.30$ achieves nearly identical F1 (0.764) while saving 26.7\%, confirming $\tau=0.3$ as the Pareto knee. At $\tau=0.35$, F1 drops to 0.726, and $\tau=0.5$ saves 72.2\% but falls to 0.619.

The main takeaway is that dense semantic query--paragraph similarity is the most informative signal we observed for distractor-heavy multi-hop QA on HotpotQA. Rank, arm identity, surface lexical overlap, and hard gap rules are all insufficient on this benchmark. A training-free method that exposes the semantic signal with a deterministic filter, as the noise gate does, matches fixed $k=10$ within tolerance while using fewer tokens---without requiring a training pipeline or per-dataset model fitting.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Pareto optimality of \texorpdfstring{$\tau{=}0.3$}{tau=0.3}.}
The five-point $\tau$ sweep (Table~\ref{tab:noise_gate_thresholds}) traces a smooth quality--cost frontier. Within the $\pm 0.05$ F1 tolerance band, the noise gate at $\tau{=}0.3$ offers the largest token reduction ($-$26.7\%), while $\tau{=}0.25$ achieves nearly identical F1 (0.765 vs.\ 0.764) at only $-$16.9\% savings. This confirms $\tau{=}0.3$ as the Pareto knee. The $\tau{=}0.2$ setting preserves tighter quality (F1~0.779, $-$9.3\%), while $\tau{=}0.35$ ($-$38.7\%) and $\tau{=}0.5$ ($-$72.2\%) trade quality for larger savings.

\paragraph{Cost implications at scale.}
On \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, the per-query cost drops from \$2.95$\times$10$^{-4}$ at $k{=}10$ to \$2.17$\times$10$^{-4}$ at $\tau{=}0.3$, a saving of \$7.8$\times$10$^{-5}$ per query; at one million queries per day, this translates to approximately 387 million fewer input tokens and roughly \$28{,}000 in annual savings. The embedding step (multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1, 384 dimensions) adds negligible inference latency ($<$2\,ms per paragraph on CPU), and embeddings for static corpora can be pre-computed and cached.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Four limitations qualify our findings.
\paragraph{(i) Limited transfer scale.} Most experiments use HotpotQA distractor. We add 100-example pilots on 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue-Ans, but these are still exploratory and do not establish full-scale transfer frontiers. Generalization to open-domain retrieval (e.g., FEVER, Natural Questions) remains untested.
\paragraph{(ii) Single generation model.} We fix \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} to isolate the context-selection variable. Stronger models may tolerate noisier context, shifting the optimal $\tau$.
\paragraph{(iii) Threshold sensitivity.} The recommended $\tau{=}0.3$ is dataset-specific. Domains with different embedding-similarity distributions may require recalibration; we do not propose a domain-adaptive mechanism. Unlike learned baselines, however, Noise-Gate requires no training pipeline and operates on pre-computed embeddings, so practitioners can tune thresholds on a small validation sample without building a full learning pipeline.
\paragraph{(iv) Bandit generalization.} Title-based UCB1 is weak on HotpotQA distractor because title overlap between training and validation is sparse. In settings with denser reward signals or a closed title vocabulary, bandit approaches may be better matched and merit further study.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Several directions follow from these results.
A hybrid architecture could apply the noise gate as a fast first-stage filter followed by a lightweight cross-encoder reranker on surviving paragraphs, combining the gate's efficiency with finer-grained relevance estimation.
An adaptive $\tau$ mechanism that selects $\tau$ as a function of the query's embedding-score distribution could remove the need for per-domain threshold tuning.
Scaling the 2WikiMultiHopQA and MuSiQue-Ans pilots to their full validation splits, and extending the evaluation to open-domain benchmarks such as FEVER, would clarify where threshold filtering remains sufficient and where learned approaches recover their advantage.
Finally, testing with stronger generation models would reveal whether the quality--cost frontier shifts when the model can better compensate for noisy context.

The bandit results suggest a narrower future direction as well: UCB1-style reranking remains a candidate family, but it likely needs either a closed title vocabulary or a better title abstraction to be competitive on HotpotQA-like benchmarks.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to answer whether test-time context compression can reduce the token cost of retrieval-augmented generation without significant quality loss. On HotpotQA distractor ($n{=}400$, \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}), fixed $k{=}10$ retrieval achieves F1~0.792 but costs $14\times$ more tokens than no retrieval (1452.5 vs.\ 103.5). We systematically evaluated five compression strategies and found that a two-stage noise gate (cosine-similarity filtering followed by Jaccard redundancy removal) achieves F1~0.764 at $\tau{=}0.3$, reducing tokens by 26.7\% while remaining within $\pm 0.05$ F1 of the baseline. No learned strategy matched this tradeoff: Adaptive-$k$ (F1~0.627), UCB1-TUNED (F1~0.704), and LinUCB (F1~0.620) all fell short.

Our analysis identifies the structural reason for this outcome on HotpotQA: sparse title overlap between training and validation and lexical indistinguishability of BM25-retrieved distractors deprive learned approaches of the signals they need to outperform a static policy, while the noise gate sidesteps these failure modes by using dense semantic similarity and a deterministic threshold with no training. The practical implication is that, for distractor-heavy QA with a fixed retrieved pool, practitioners can reduce token cost with a training-free, interpretable filter whose only tunable parameter~($\tau$) admits a clear Pareto interpretation---and whose thresholds can be recalibrated on a small validation sample without a full training pipeline. In HotpotQA distractor, embedding-based threshold filtering is the most effective approach we found; in the transfer pilots, 2Wiki improves cleanly while MuSiQue confirms that optimal gate parameters are benchmark-dependent, a design variable that must be accounted for in deployment.
