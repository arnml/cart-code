\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems typically retrieve a fixed number of documents per query, spending tokens on irrelevant context. On HotpotQA distractor, where each query includes 2 gold and 8 distractor paragraphs, fixed $k{=}10$ retrieval achieves high F1 but costs roughly $14\times$ more tokens than answering without retrieval. We propose Noise-Gated Retrieval, a training-free test-time context-compression method that combines cosine-similarity and Jaccard-overlap filtering to selectively retain only high-utility documents. We evaluate our method against four alternative strategies on 400 matched validation examples: fixed-$k$ baselines, Adaptive-$k$ similarity-gap truncation, title-based UCB1-TUNED bandit selection, and LinUCB contextual bandits with lexical features. Noise-Gated Retrieval achieves the best observed quality-cost tradeoff, reducing tokens by 26.7\% while remaining within 0.03 F1 of the $k{=}10$ baseline. We further show that learned strategies fail to outperform our approach due to three structural properties of distractor-heavy multi-hop QA: topically adjacent BM25 distractors, sparse title overlap between training and inference, and semantic proximity between distractors and true evidence. These results indicate that embedding-based threshold filtering is a practical test-time controller for semantically dense retrieval settings.

\keywords{Retrieval-Augmented Generation \and Test-time Compression \and Context Filtering \and Noise Gating}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% Para 1: Problem Statement (Hook with inefficiency)
Most deployed retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems pass a fixed number of retrieved documents to the language model for every query~\cite{lewis2020rag,klesel2025rag}. This fixed top-$k$ policy is straightforward to implement but often wasteful: it spends tokens on redundant or irrelevant passages even when fewer documents would suffice, and recent studies confirm that noisy context can actively degrade answer quality~\cite{verma2026reflectiverag,li2025bestpractices}. As RAG adoption scales, this per-query overhead compounds into a significant inference-cost bottleneck.

% Para 2: Specific problem + Research question + Early novelty signal
We study this problem on HotpotQA distractor~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, a multi-hop question-answering benchmark where each example provides exactly 10 paragraphs (2 gold paragraphs and 8 distractors). On GPT-5.4-mini, fixed $k{=}10$ retrieval achieves F1\,=\,0.792 but costs 1452.5 mean tokens per example: roughly $14\times$ the cost of answering without retrieval (103.5 tokens, F1\,=\,0.387). The research question is therefore: \emph{can we compress the retrieved context at inference time without significant quality loss?} All three learned strategies, Adaptive-$k$, title-based UCB1-TUNED, and LinUCB, underperform the threshold-based filter.

% Para 3: Why naive approaches fail (3 structural properties)
Three structural properties of the benchmark explain why context selection is non-obvious and why learned approaches struggle. First, distractors are not random noise: they are retrieved by BM25 precisely because they are topically adjacent to the question, so their embeddings closely match those of true supporting facts~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. Second, the candidate pool has a finite title vocabulary, but title overlap between training and validation is sparse, so title-level reward tracking faces frequent cold-start. Third, multi-hop reasoning requires mutually complementary evidence sets~\cite{trivedi2023interleave}, so the problem is not merely to identify individually relevant passages but to select documents that jointly support a reasoning chain.

% Para 4: Systematic evaluation + Key finding + Failure modes
We find that embedding-based threshold filtering outperforms learned bandit strategies on this benchmark. In systematic evaluation on 400 matched validation examples, our proposed \emph{noise-gate} (combining cosine-similarity and Jaccard-overlap filtering) achieves 26.7\% token reduction while maintaining F1 within $\pm 0.05$ of the $k{=}10$ baseline (F1\,=\,0.764 at $\tau{=}0.3$, $\rho{=}0.65$). We evaluate three fixed baselines ($k \in \{3, 5, 10\}$), Adaptive-$k$ similarity-gap truncation~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, title-based UCB1-TUNED bandit selection, and LinUCB contextual bandit with lexical features. We trace each learned method's underperformance to a specific structural cause: hard similarity gaps can split distractor clusters from evidence in Adaptive-$k$, sparse title overlap and cold-start undermine title-based reward tracking in UCB1-TUNED, and semantic indistinguishability between distractors and gold passages defeats lexical-feature regression in LinUCB.

% Para 5: Contributions (focused on 2 core + 1 supporting)
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Noise-Gate}, a training-free two-stage embedding filter that achieves $-$26.7\% token reduction with minimal quality loss (F1\,=\,0.764 vs.\ 0.792), validated across ablations over cosine threshold $\tau \in \{0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35, 0.5\}$ and Jaccard threshold $\rho \in \{0.50, 0.55, 0.65\}$.
    \item \textbf{Failure analysis} of three learned strategies (Adaptive-$k$, UCB1-TUNED, LinUCB) on distractor-heavy multi-hop QA, identifying the structural properties that make this problem class resistant to similarity-gap, title-based, and feature-based learning.
    \item \textbf{Systematic comparison} of five context-compression strategies on a matched sample ($n{=}400$, GPT-5.4-mini), providing a reproducible benchmark for future work on test-time context selection.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Fixed-budget retrieval.}
Foundational RAG systems retrieve the top-$k$ documents by relevance~\cite{lewis2020rag}. Fixed $k$ remains the dominant approach in deployed systems~\cite{klesel2025rag}. Our work takes fixed $k=10$ retrieval as the quality baseline and asks how to retain its quality at lower token cost.

\paragraph{Adaptive retrieval control.}
Self-RAG~\cite{asai2024selfrag}, DioR~\cite{guo2025dior}, and DRAGIN~\cite{su2024dragin} learn when to retrieve and how much to retrieve using training or learned tokens. These methods require additional training or model modification. Our work is orthogonal: we focus on filtering a fixed retrieved pool without training, a test-time-only strategy.

\paragraph{Context noise and compression.}
Recent work addresses context quality by selecting subsets of retrieved documents. Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} selects documents by similarity-score gaps; we evaluate it as a representative of gap-based strategies. SetR~\cite{lee2025setr} and RankRAG~\cite{yu2024rankrag} select context as a set using complementarity and downstream usefulness. ReflectiveRAG~\cite{verma2026reflectiverag} shows that irrelevant or redundant context can hurt quality. Our noise-gate component is conceptually related to Maximum Marginal Relevance~\cite{goldstein1998mmr}, which trades off relevance and diversity, but uses a two-stage design: cosine similarity for relevance, Jaccard overlap for redundancy.

\paragraph{Bandits and learning-to-rank.}
Bandit algorithms (UCB, Thompson Sampling, and contextual bandit methods such as LinUCB) are classical methods from online decision-making, ranking, and contextual recommendation, rather than RAG-specific retrieval controllers~\cite{auer2002ucb1,thompson1933,chu2011linucb}. We test whether UCB1-TUNED and a LinUCB-style contextual bandit can transfer to context-selection on HotpotQA distractor, finding that they do not under this benchmark's structure. We characterize those failure modes in a concrete setting.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $q$ be a question and let $D = (d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_{10})$ denote the fixed HotpotQA distractor context for that question: two gold paragraphs and eight distractor paragraphs, ordered as provided by the benchmark. A context-selection strategy returns an ordered subsequence $S \preceq D$ to be concatenated with $q$ and passed to the reader model. We do not control retrieval itself; the candidate pool is fixed, and the only decision is which paragraphs to retain.

Let $\hat{a} = g(q, S)$ be the generated answer and let $a^*$ be the reference answer. We evaluate quality with the standard HotpotQA normalized token-level F1 and exact match metrics, as implemented in our evaluation code. To trade off quality against inference cost, we define
\[
J(S) = F_1(\hat{a}, a^*) - \lambda \, C(q, S),
\]
where $C(q, S)$ is the total token cost of the model call, computed as input tokens plus output tokens, and $\lambda > 0$ is a cost weight. In the experiments we report token-level F1 as the primary quality metric, exact match as a secondary metric, and mean total tokens per question as the cost metric.

\section{Methods}

We study test-time context compression through five strategies, spanning fixed baselines, learned rerankers, and a training-free threshold filter. The methods differ along two axes: question-level vs.\ section-level control, and training-free vs.\ training-required selection.

\subsection{Baselines}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No retrieval} ($k{=}0$): Answer from parametric knowledge only (103.5 mean tokens, F1=0.387).
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}3$}: Top-3 retrieved documents (471.3 tokens, F1=0.651).
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}5$}: Top-5 retrieved documents (731.7 tokens, F1=0.716).
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}10$}: All 10 retrieved documents (1452.5 tokens, F1=0.792).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adaptive-$k$ (Similarity-Gap Truncation)}

Given retrieved documents with similarity scores $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \cdots \ge s_{10}$, Adaptive-$k$~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek} selects the cut point of maximum adjacent separation:
\begin{equation}
k^* = \arg\max_{1 \le i < 10} (s_i - s_{i+1}).
\end{equation}
The method assumes that a large embedding-similarity gap separates relevant context from noise. On HotpotQA distractor, distractors are intentionally topically close to the gold evidence, so the largest gap often falls inside a cluster of distractors rather than at the boundary between evidence and noise.

\subsection{UCB1-TUNED (Title-Based Bandit)}

We model paragraph titles as arms and estimate their utility from the full training split ($n=90{,}447$ HotpotQA distractor examples). For each title, we maintain the empirical mean reward and variance using Welford's online algorithm. The reward is binary: 1.0 if the title appears in supporting\_facts, and 0.0 otherwise. At inference, each title is scored by UCB1-TUNED:
\[
\mathrm{score}(\text{title}) = \mu_{\text{title}} + \sqrt{\frac{\ln T}{n_{\text{title}}} \cdot \min(0.25, \sigma_{\text{title}}^2)}.
\]
The reranker then selects the top-$k$ titles by this score. Unseen titles fall back to BM25 ranking.

\subsection{LinUCB (Contextual Bandit with Lexical Features)}

This variant models each question--document pair with a three-dimensional feature vector
\[
x(q, d) = [f_1, f_2, f_3]^\top,
\]
where $f_1$ is the fraction of title words appearing in the question, $f_2$ is the normalized question length, and $f_3$ is the normalized section length. Given the training observations $\{(x_t, r_t)\}_{t=1}^T$, with binary reward $r_t \in \{0,1\}$, we estimate
\[
\hat{\theta} = A^{-1} b,
\qquad
A = I + \sum_{t=1}^T x_t x_t^\top,
\qquad
b = \sum_{t=1}^T r_t x_t.
\]
At inference, each candidate is scored by the usual LinUCB rule
\[
p(q, d) = \hat{\theta}^\top x(q, d) + \alpha \sqrt{x(q, d)^\top A^{-1} x(q, d)},
\]
and the top-$k$ documents are selected by this score. Unlike a rank-position bandit, this formulation attempts to learn document-level utility directly from lexical and length-based features.

\subsection{Noise-Gate (Two-Stage Embedding Filter)}
\label{sec:methods_noisegate}

Our proposed method is a two-stage, training-free gate that operates directly on the fixed HotpotQA candidate pool. It is the main mechanism studied in this paper.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:noisegate} summarizes the procedure.

Given a question $q$ and retrieved paragraphs $D = (d_1, \ldots, d_{10})$, the gate first scores each paragraph by cosine similarity to the question embedding and retains only those above a threshold $\tau$.

\textbf{Stage 1 (Cosine filter):} Remove paragraphs whose cosine similarity to the question falls below threshold $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{cos}(q, d_i) = \frac{e_q \cdot e_i}{\|e_q\| \, \|e_i\|} \ge \tau.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Stage 2 (Jaccard filter):} Among paragraphs surviving Stage 1, remove those whose word-overlap with any already-kept paragraph exceeds threshold $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
\max_{d_j \in \text{kept}} \frac{|d_i \cap d_j|}{|d_i \cup d_j|} \le \rho.
\end{equation}

This two-stage rule is interpretable and designed to preserve semantically useful context while removing redundant paragraphs. We evaluate $\tau \in \{0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.35, 0.5\}$ and $\rho \in \{0.50, 0.55, 0.65\}$. Embeddings are computed using \texttt{multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1} (384 dimensions, pre-trained on QA corpora).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Noise-Gate context selection.}
\label{alg:noisegate}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Question $q$, paragraph pool $D = (d_1, \ldots, d_{10})$, thresholds $\tau$ and $\rho$
\State Compute embedding $e_q$ for $q$
\State Compute embedding $e_i$ for each $d_i \in D$
\State Rank paragraphs by cosine similarity $\cos(e_q, e_i)$ in descending order
\State $S \leftarrow [\,]$
\For{each paragraph $d_i$ in ranked order}
    \If{$\cos(e_q, e_i) < \tau$}
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \If{$\max_{d_j \in S} \mathrm{Jaccard}(d_i, d_j) > \rho$}
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \State append $d_i$ to $S$
\EndFor
\State \Return $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{Dataset.}
We evaluate on HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa}, distractor subset, validation split. Each example contains exactly 10 paragraphs: 2 gold paragraphs containing the supporting facts and 8 BM25 distractors. We use a fixed random sample of $n{=}400$ examples for all experiments, large enough to stabilize mean F1 and EM while remaining practical for matched-sample ablations.

\textbf{Model.}
All experiments use \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} as the single generation model. Fixing the model controls for tokenization, pricing, and provider-specific prompting behavior, so the comparison isolates context-selection strategy.

\textbf{Metrics.}
We report token-level F1 (primary), exact match (EM), and mean total tokens per example. F1 measures answer quality; total tokens measure the cost of the context passed to the model. We define a practical tolerance of $\pm 0.05$~F1 relative to the $k{=}10$ baseline: strategies within this band are considered quality-preserving.

\textbf{Baselines.}
We compare against three fixed retrieval policies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No retrieval} ($k{=}0$): the model answers from parametric knowledge only.
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}5$}: the top-5 BM25-ranked paragraphs are passed as context.
    \item \textbf{Fixed $k{=}10$}: all 10 paragraphs are passed (quality upper bound).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Strategies under evaluation.}
In addition to the baselines, we evaluate four context-compression strategies, all operating on the same fixed pool of 10 retrieved paragraphs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive-$k$}: a similarity-gap truncation rule that selects the cut point from question features and keeps the top-$k^*$ paragraphs by embedding similarity.
    \item \textbf{UCB1-TUNED}: a title-based bandit reranker that tracks per-title reward statistics over the training split and selects the top-$k$ titles by UCB score at inference. Unseen titles fall back to BM25 ranking.
    \item \textbf{LinUCB}: a contextual bandit that uses lexical features (token overlap, title-in-question fraction, section length) and ridge-regression reward estimation with an exploration bonus.
    \item \textbf{Noise-Gate} (proposed): the two-stage filter from Section~\ref{sec:methods_noisegate}. No training required.
\end{itemize}
For the bandit strategies (UCB1-TUNED and LinUCB), we report results at $k{=}5$ to allow direct comparison with the fixed $k{=}5$ baseline at equal retrieval budget.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{subsec:main_results}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} presents the unified comparison across all strategies. The fixed $k{=}10$ baseline achieves F1~0.792 with 1452.5 mean total tokens, about $14\times$ more tokens than no retrieval ($k{=}0$, 103.5 tokens). \section{Analysis: Why Learned Methods Fail on Distractor-Heavy Multi-Hop QA}
\label{sec:analysis}

We test three learned context-selection strategies on HotpotQA distractor: Adaptive-$k$, title-based UCB1-TUNED, and LinUCB with lexical features. In every head-to-head comparison, the zero-training noise gate matches or exceeds them. The outcome is structural rather than accidental: distractors are topically deceptive, title identity is sparse across train and validation, and lexical features are confusable.

\subsection{Adaptive-$k$: similarity gaps place the cut in the wrong cluster}
\label{subsec:fail_adaptivek}

The Adaptive-$k$ rule truncates the retrieved list at the largest adjacent similarity gap~\cite{taguchi2025adaptivek}, under the implicit assumption that embedding-similarity rank is monotone in true relevance. In HotpotQA distractor, that assumption is too weak to be useful. Adaptive-$k$ selects compact contexts (mean $k^*=3.02$, mean total tokens 483.1) but reaches only F1 0.627, below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k3} (0.651), \texttt{retrieval\_k5} (0.716), and \texttt{retrieval\_k10} (0.792).

The reason is dataset-specific. HotpotQA distractors are BM25-selected to be topically adjacent to the gold evidence, so they share entities, vocabulary, and discourse style with the question. Their embedding-similarity scores therefore cluster close to those of the true supporting facts, and the largest gap often falls inside a distractor cluster rather than at the evidence boundary. The top-$k^*$ paragraphs then exclude genuine evidence while retaining topical but unhelpful context.

\subsection{UCB1-TUNED: title coverage is sparse across train and validation}
\label{subsec:fail_ucb1}

We explored whether a bandit reranker could learn which retrieved items are historically valuable. In this title-based UCB1 formulation, each paragraph title is treated as an arm, and the model tracks per-title reward statistics from the training split. On HotpotQA distractor, however, title coverage is sparse enough that cold-start is common. Table~\ref{tab:ucb1_title_coldstart} reports the cold-start behavior of this variant on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} with matched samples of $n=400$ per $k$: at $k=5$, $83.8\%$ of validation queries touch titles unseen during training, so many selections rely on BM25 fallback. At $k=2$ and $k=3$, cold-start rates are $61.5\%$ and $75.2\%$, respectively.

The resulting quality reflects this: overall F1 aggregated across $k\in\{2,3,5\}$ is 0.623, and the best single setting ($k=5$) reaches F1 0.704, still below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k5} at 0.716. The underlying issue is that HotpotQA draws questions from a Wikipedia-scale title distribution, so the training-time and validation-time title sets overlap only sparsely. This is not a failure of BM25 retrieval itself, but it does show that title-level bandit variants need a closed vocabulary or an explicit fallback policy to be practical on this benchmark.

\subsection{LinUCB: lexical features cannot distinguish crafted distractors}
\label{subsec:fail_linucb}

LinUCB addresses the cold-start problem of arm-indexed bandits by parameterizing rewards as a linear function of context features, so that previously unseen arms can still be scored. We instantiated it with three lexical and structural features: token overlap with the question, title-in-question fraction, and section length. LinUCB achieves an overall F1 of 0.550, underperforming fixed $k{=}3$ (0.651, gap of $-$0.101) and fixed $k{=}5$ (0.716, gap of $-$0.166) despite using only 494.8 mean tokens. The failure mechanism parallels Adaptive-$k$ but at the feature level. HotpotQA distractors are themselves retrieved by BM25, a lexical scoring function, so distractors and supporting facts overlap heavily in surface tokens, title words, and section length. On these features, the two populations are nearly indistinguishable, and a linear model cannot separate them.

A dense semantic feature, such as the cosine similarity between pre-trained sentence embeddings of the query and paragraph, would partially break this symmetry. However, computing dense embeddings for the full training split on CPU is impractical at this scale, so LinUCB remains limited to lexical features. As a result, it cannot recover the semantic signal needed to distinguish crafted distractors from genuine supporting facts.

\subsection{Why the noise gate succeeds}
\label{subsec:why_noise_gate}

The three failure modes above share a single underlying cause: the signals used by learned policies are not discriminative in HotpotQA distractor. Title identity is non-stationary between training and validation, and lexical features are matched by construction because distractors are selected lexically. The noise gate avoids these problems with a single deterministic rule applied at inference time: it uses dense sentence embeddings, and its cutoff is a per-paragraph threshold rather than a single largest-gap cut. Table~\ref{tab:failure_matrix} summarizes the correspondence.

\toprule
Failure mode & Affected strategy & Noise-gate response \\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The quantitative payoff is visible at a single operating point. At $\tau=0.3$ and $\rho=0.65$, the noise gate reaches F1 0.764 on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} ($n=400$), only 0.028 below fixed \texttt{retrieval\_k10} at 0.792. At the same time, mean total tokens drop to 1065.3, a 26.7\% reduction relative to \texttt{retrieval\_k10} at 1452.5. None of the learned strategies approaches this tradeoff: Adaptive-$k$ falls more than 0.15 F1 below \texttt{retrieval\_k10}, title-based UCB1-TUNED at $k{=}5$ reaches 0.704 but falls back to BM25 on 83.8\% of queries, and LinUCB at $k{=}5$ reaches 0.620 under the same budget.

The full five-point $\tau$ sweep (Table~\ref{tab:noise_gate_thresholds}) clarifies why $\tau=0.3$ is the chosen operating point. The lenient setting $\tau=0.2$ achieves higher F1 (0.779) but only a 9.3\% token reduction. The intermediate $\tau=0.25$ reaches F1~0.765 with 16.9\% savings, and $\tau=0.30$ achieves nearly identical F1 (0.764) while saving 26.7\%, confirming $\tau=0.3$ as the Pareto knee. At $\tau=0.35$, F1 drops to 0.726, and $\tau=0.5$ saves 72.2\% but falls to 0.619.

The main takeaway is that dense semantic query--paragraph similarity is the most informative signal we observed for distractor-heavy multi-hop QA. Rank, arm identity, surface lexical overlap, and hard gap rules are all insufficient. A method that exposes the semantic signal with a deterministic filter, as the noise gate does, matches fixed $k=10$ within tolerance while using fewer tokens.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Pareto optimality of \texorpdfstring{$\tau{=}0.3$}{tau=0.3}.}
The five-point $\tau$ sweep (Table~\ref{tab:noise_gate_thresholds}) traces a smooth quality--cost frontier. Within the $\pm 0.05$ F1 tolerance band, the noise gate at $\tau{=}0.3$ offers the largest token reduction ($-$26.7\%), while $\tau{=}0.25$ achieves nearly identical F1 (0.765 vs.\ 0.764) at only $-$16.9\% savings. This confirms $\tau{=}0.3$ as the Pareto knee. The $\tau{=}0.2$ setting preserves tighter quality (F1~0.779, $-$9.3\%), while $\tau{=}0.35$ ($-$38.7\%) and $\tau{=}0.5$ ($-$72.2\%) trade quality for larger savings.

\paragraph{Cost implications at scale.}
On \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, the per-query cost drops from \$2.95$\times$10$^{-4}$ at $k{=}10$ to \$2.17$\times$10$^{-4}$ at $\tau{=}0.3$, a saving of \$7.8$\times$10$^{-5}$ per query; at one million queries per day, this translates to approximately 387 million fewer input tokens and roughly \$28{,}000 in annual savings. The embedding step (multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1, 384 dimensions) adds negligible inference latency ($<$2\,ms per paragraph on CPU), and embeddings for static corpora can be pre-computed and cached.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Four limitations qualify our findings.
\emph{(i) Single benchmark.} All experiments use HotpotQA distractor. This benchmark has specific structural properties (exactly 2 gold paragraphs among 8 BM25-retrieved distractors) that favour threshold-based filtering. Generalization to benchmarks with variable gold rates (e.g., MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA) or open-domain retrieval (e.g., FEVER, Natural Questions) is untested.
\emph{(ii) Single generation model.} We fix \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini} to isolate the context-selection variable. Stronger models may tolerate noisier context, shifting the optimal $\tau$.
\emph{(iii) Threshold sensitivity.} The recommended $\tau{=}0.3$ is dataset-specific. Domains with different embedding-similarity distributions may require recalibration; we do not propose a domain-adaptive mechanism.
\emph{(iv) Bandit generalization.} Title-based UCB1 is weak on HotpotQA distractor because title overlap between training and validation is sparse. In settings with denser reward signals or a closed title vocabulary, bandit approaches may be better matched and merit further study.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Several directions follow from these results.
A hybrid architecture could apply the noise gate as a fast first-stage filter followed by a lightweight cross-encoder reranker on surviving paragraphs, combining the gate's efficiency with finer-grained relevance estimation.
An adaptive $\tau$ mechanism that selects $\tau$ as a function of the query's embedding-score distribution could remove the need for per-domain threshold tuning.
Extending the evaluation to additional benchmarks (MuSiQue, 2WikiMultiHopQA, FEVER) would clarify where threshold filtering remains sufficient and where learned approaches recover their advantage.
Finally, testing with stronger generation models would reveal whether the quality--cost frontier shifts when the model can better compensate for noisy context.

The bandit results suggest a narrower future direction as well: UCB1-style reranking remains a candidate family, but it likely needs either a closed title vocabulary or a better title abstraction to be competitive on HotpotQA-like benchmarks.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to answer whether test-time context compression can reduce the token cost of retrieval-augmented generation without significant quality loss. On HotpotQA distractor ($n{=}400$, \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}), fixed $k{=}10$ retrieval achieves F1~0.792 but costs $14\times$ more tokens than no retrieval (1452.5 vs.\ 103.5). We systematically evaluated five compression strategies and found that a two-stage noise gate (cosine-similarity filtering followed by Jaccard redundancy removal) achieves F1~0.764 at $\tau{=}0.3$, reducing tokens by 26.7\% while remaining within $\pm 0.05$ F1 of the baseline. No learned strategy matched this tradeoff: Adaptive-$k$ (F1~0.627), UCB1-TUNED (F1~0.704), and LinUCB (F1~0.620) all fell short.

Our analysis identifies the structural reason for this outcome: sparse title overlap between training and validation and lexical indistinguishability of BM25-retrieved distractors deprive learned approaches of the signals they need to outperform a static policy, while the noise gate sidesteps these failure modes by using dense semantic similarity and a deterministic threshold with no training. The practical implication is that, for distractor-heavy QA with a fixed retrieved pool, practitioners can reduce token cost with a training-free, interpretable filter whose only tunable parameter~($\tau$) admits a clear Pareto interpretation. In HotpotQA distractor, embedding-based threshold filtering is the most effective approach we found.
